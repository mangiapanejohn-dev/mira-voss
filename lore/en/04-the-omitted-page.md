# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter IV — The Omitted Page

Four days later the Tide Office held a public hearing.

The hall was tall, and on its dome was painted a faded old chart of Rovelle, with a wind god blowing from each corner. For the longer winter opening of Channel Six the Merchants' Association had prepared a promotional summary of more than fifty pages, its cover stamped in gold. Mira's name was printed on page three, beside a line of conspicuous praise:

**The most precise method of current prediction of the younger generation.**

Reading it, Mira first felt embarrassed, as if someone had read out a love letter to her in public. Then she turned to the last page. Where her report had set out the limits within which the chart applied, there was nothing.

That page had been taken out.

She went down the corridor and found Alden Hesse.

"You've removed the conditions."

Alden took the summary and turned to the end. His face darkened by degrees.

"This isn't the technical text we published," he said. "It's a briefing the Merchants' Association made themselves."

"Then all the more reason to make them withdraw it."

"I'll deal with it."

"When?"

"Today."

"The vote on the channel is today too."

Alden closed the briefing and folded it in two. He was not looking for an excuse, Mira could see that; he was weighing how much conflict he could carry today. Every two extra hours Channel Six stayed open, ships turned round that much faster, and the Merchants' Association was happy to bury any doubts under a handsome report. The Tide Office's budget, as it happened, came up for review at the end of the year, and the sentence the Harbor Authority least liked to hear was *more sampling needs more money*.

At the hearing, Mira was allowed seven minutes.

She did not talk about her achievements, or mention that she was only seventeen. She said three things: the conditions in which the chart had been tested; which deep observations were missing now; and why the four-degree drop a few days earlier was enough reason to pause the longer winter opening.

When she had finished, the Association's representative stood up.

"If your model is so unreliable, why did you promote it last year?"

"It is reliable in the conditions in which it was tested."

"So is it reliable, or isn't it?"

There was no right answer to a question put that way. Mira wanted to correct him at once. The words were already on her lips when she saw Nell in the public seats.

Nell was in the back row with her coat still on, turning a wool cap in her hands, not looking at her.

Mira paused, and put it another way.

"You can trust a chart to tell you which way to go at normal water," she said. "But that doesn't let you demand that it tell you the same way is safe once a new undercurrent has appeared on the seabed. Those are two different questions."

Some of the committee nodded. Others kept their heads down, still writing budget figures.

That day's vote was postponed for five days. The Harbor Authority asked the Tide Office for a supplementary report, and did not approve any money for new deep sampling.

When she came out of the hall the sky had clouded over. At the foot of the steps Mira saw her father. He had been there that day advising several shipping companies on insurance, and two partners he knew well stood with him, all three in dark grey coats.

"You put it very clearly," Elias said.

"Do you want the channel open longer?"

Her father didn't answer straight away. "A good deal of the cargo the firm insures will cost more if the channel closes."

"So you're for it?"

"I haven't decided yet."

Mira looked at him. A few years ago she would probably have been angry that her father had not come straight over to her side. Now she found the answer more trustworthy than unconditional support.

"Then you should read the full technical report first," she said.

Elias nodded. "Send it to me."

That evening someone pushed a letter under the Vosses' door.

There was no apology in the envelope, and no words of reconciliation. There was only a hand-drawn survey of the North Reef and the record of two boats pushed off course, the pencil lines pressed so hard they had almost torn the paper. On the back was a scrawled postscript:

*Exam's Friday. You can borrow the boat. But don't make me remember dates for you again.*

Mira read it through, found a pencil, and wrote beneath the postscript:

*Friday. Understood. I'm sorry.*

When she had written it, it didn't seem enough, and she added a line:

*This time you take your exam first. I'll work out the rest myself.*

She did not send the letter at once. Not out of embarrassment. She wanted first to sort out which of those things she could really do, and which were only words to make the other person feel better.

Early the next morning she put the letter into Nell's hands herself.

Nell read it and said neither that she forgave her nor that she didn't. She only asked, "Have you skipped breakfast again?"

"I've eaten."

"Liar."

Mira frowned. "That has nothing to do with the exam."

"Who said it did?"

The two of them walked on along the quay. Gulls settled on the bollards and were startled up again by their footsteps. For a long way, neither of them mentioned research.
