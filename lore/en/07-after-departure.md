# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter VII — After Departure

The day Mira left Rovelle, it did not rain.

The sky was very pale, like a chart washed too many times. The early cold light turned the wooden jetty at the Old Salt Docks silver-grey, and a thin frost had formed in the cracks between the boards, creaking underfoot. She took only two cases: one for clothes and everyday things, the other for her research notebooks, instrument parts, and a dozen rolls of old charts gone brittle at the edges.

Her parents insisted on seeing her to the boat station, but they did not say they would go with her to Ailan, and they did not tell her over and over to be careful. On the platform they asked a few practical things: the ticket, the lodgings, what time she would arrive. She answered each one clearly.

Just before she boarded, her mother pressed a brown paper bag into her hands: salt bread and a few slices of dried sour orange. Mira was about to say there would be food on the boat, but she saw that her mother had already folded the top of the bag over twice and pressed it flat, so she said nothing.

"When I get there, I'll write," she said.

Elias nodded. Lena reached out and held her. The embrace was a little careful, as if asking her permission. Mira did not step back, nor did she suddenly become good at returning it. She touched her mother's back lightly, then let go.

For today, that was enough.

Nell arrived eleven minutes later than they had agreed. She had a long canvas bag on her back and was cursing the late tram as she ran, a roll of new chart under her arm about to come undone.

"What have you got this time?" Mira asked.

"Something of yours."

Nell spread the chart on the jetty rail and held it down with both elbows. It was the inshore chart of the North Reef that the pilots had re-surveyed over the past two weeks. Its margins were crowded with handwriting, a jumble of it: beside every sample point was the name of the person responsible for it. People from the Tide Office, from the boat sheds, from the boats, all squeezed together, some in ink, some in pencil; one name had been written crooked, crossed out and written again.

"A new chart can't carry only the researchers' names," Nell said. "I thought you'd like it."

Mira did not speak at once.

She did like it. She liked it so much she didn't know what to say.

"Aren't you supposed to be resting after your exam?"

"I rested for two days."

"That's not the point."

"Then what is?"

Mira rolled the chart up carefully and tied it with the thin cord she always carried. Then she said:

"Thank you."

Nell stood there as though she had never heard the words before.

"Have you got a fever?"

"No."

"Then say it again."

"I won't."

They both laughed.

The whistle sounded for the first time. Nell asked suddenly, "Will you come back?"

Mira glanced at the boat. She didn't know. The station contract was for a year; it might be extended. She wanted to go to Ailan, and further north to Ice Bay, to see the waters that were only a few pencil lines on a chart. She also wanted to know whether, once she had finally left everything familiar behind, she would become someone else.

"I don't know," she said.

Nell nodded, and there was no disappointment in her face.

"Then write."

"I will."

This time the words did not catch in her throat.

The boat drew slowly away from the shore. The houses on Cape Ward folded into one pale shape in the thin mist, like a draft no one had inked in yet. The bell of the Outer Harbor came after them over the water, once, and again, fainter and fainter. Mira stood on deck and opened her mother's paper bag and ate a slice of the dried sour orange first. It was far too sweet. She finished it anyway.

She thought of the clocks she had set seven minutes slow when she was nine; of the waxed corridor at the Review when she was thirteen; of the report that came back, the post office about to close, Nell's old jacket that smelled of tung oil, and the observatory window that leaked whenever it rained.

In these years she had done many things she had thought would take much longer to do. She had also spent a great deal of time proving, correcting, working, and going on. Now she was going further away, to do harder research that no one would necessarily praise.

She had not stopped because of what she had already done.

Nor, because she had to keep going, had she treated the past as a pile of old paper to throw away.

Someone had once asked her whether being that kind of genius meant she could do everything on her own.

If she were asked again now, she thought, she would say: no. It was only that many things happened to be done by her first, and later, slowly, she learned to let other people in.

But that was too long. It didn't sound like something she would say.

She laid Nell's chart on her knees and wrote a line in small letters in the blank space:

*North Reef, third observation station: to be measured again.*

She thought for a moment, then wrote two names beneath it.

The boat went on north.

No miracle appeared on the sea, and no bell in the distance announced a new life for her. Rovelle only grew smaller, little by little, until the grey weather took it in. She knew the city would not stop because she had left. People at the Old Salt Docks would still go out, quarrel, sell bread and mend boats; and Nell would keep going too.

After a long while, Mira took from her pocket the invitation, folded so often it had gone soft.

She slipped it into the innermost fold of the new chart.

On that page, she had drawn no channels.

Some places can only be charted after someone arrives.
