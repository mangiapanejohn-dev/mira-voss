# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter II — The Unasked Question

At thirteen, Mira went to the North Shore Survey Review for the first time, in a dark coat that did not quite fit.

Her mother had ordered it from the tailor three weeks ahead and had it sent over. The sleeves were too long and the shoulders too narrow; when she raised her arm to write, a crease pulled tight across her back. That day her mother was out of town looking at the building site of a hospital, and her father was dealing with insurance claims after a storm. The one who went with her to Ailan was the family driver.

The Review was held in an old school. The corridor was long and the floor freshly waxed, with a shine that felt unsteady underfoot. Mira spread her tide chart on the table, with seventeen pages of derivation beside it and a stack of raw records. All the way there she had worried that her material was not enough, that she had not carried the error on some page all the way through. It had never occurred to her that someone would ask first:

"Little girl, which university is your father a professor at?"

"He's in insurance," she said.

"Then who wrote it for you?"

Somebody in the classroom gave a small laugh.

She stood there without moving for perhaps three seconds. Then she went to the blackboard, picked up the chalk, and wrote out the whole derivation again from the first line. The chalk broke twice. She did not turn round to see who it was.

When she finished, her fingers and her cuffs were white. The reviewers stopped asking who the author was and began arguing about the error in mounting the tide staff that she had left out. Somebody stood up and drew a circle beside her ninth line.

That was exactly the discussion she had wanted. She forgot the question almost at once.

When the results came out she had the highest grade the Review gives. Her father telephoned long distance to say congratulations, and that he had already chosen her present: a complete set of new surveying instruments imported from the south.

"Do you need anything else?" he asked.

"No."

She truly thought so at the time.

On the boat back to Rovelle the driver was seasick and went to his cabin early. Mira sat alone on a bench in the passage, reading the reviewers' notes in her margins over and over. Outside the porthole the sea went from grey to dark and from dark to black, until there was nothing in the glass but her own face.

Nobody had asked her whether she was afraid, standing at the blackboard. Nobody had listened to how angry *who wrote it for you* had made her. It had not occurred to her to telephone anyone and say so.

She filed all of it in a drawer: the drawer kept for things she could handle herself. Over the years the drawer grew larger, and she grew better and better at handling them.

At fourteen a report of hers was sent back because the reviewers suspected an adult had written it. She packed every raw record, every page of working, every dated draft into a wooden crate and sent it to them. A month later they withdrew the objection, without an apology. In a corner of her notebook she wrote: *From now on, every public conclusion goes out with the way to check it.*

At fifteen a report she had written with Rowan was sent back to be rewritten because it had underestimated extreme weather. She spent four months reworking the observations, sleeping very little for weeks at a stretch, so short-tempered that even Nell couldn't be bothered with her. On the evening the fair copy of the revision was finally done, she ran to the post office, and it had just closed.

She stood outside the door. Rain ran off the eaves and soaked the brown paper round the manuscript she held against her chest. At that moment she badly wanted to walk down to the quay and throw the whole project into the harbor and watch it slowly sink.

It was Nell who came out of the boat shed across the road and found out for her about the mail bag for the first boat in the morning.

"If you keep staring at that door," Nell said, "it isn't going to open because you glared at it."

Mira was tired and cross. "I know."

Nell didn't argue the point. She only took off her own dry jacket and put it round Mira's shoulders.

Many years later Mira still remembered the smell of tung oil and tar on that jacket, and the half sheet of unused buoy chart in its pocket, its corner curled. She did not remember very well how she had felt when the report was finally accepted.

At sixteen she signed her first proper research contract. The fee would have satisfied a grown observer. On the day she signed, both her parents made a point of being home, and many candles were lit in the dining room.

Her mother asked whether she could stay up a little less late. Her father said they could hire an assistant to manage her diary.

She put down her fork.

"I've been arranging these things myself since I was fourteen."

"We're only worried about you," her mother said.

"Then you can just say you're worried," she said. "You don't have to arrange things for me."

The candle flames wavered. The table was quiet for a while. Later her father brought up the liability clause in the contract, and on that Mira was glad to talk; she set out the risks, the payments and the ownership of the results clearly, and her father listened closely. For the second half of dinner the family even got on well.

But coming out of the dining room and climbing the stairs, she felt very tired.

It was hard to tell them that she did not object to being cared for; she only did not want to be led by the hand again once she had learned to stand. Harder to say was that she still sometimes thought of the passage on the boat when she was thirteen: if someone had sat down beside her then and asked, *How are you today?*, what would she have answered?

Nobody ever asked.

So she would never know the answer.
