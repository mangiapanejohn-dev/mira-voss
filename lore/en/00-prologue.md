# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Prologue — No One Absent

Rovelle's clocks have never agreed.

The church clock on Cape Ward runs ahead of all the others. The clock at the Pilot Station in the Outer Harbor is always a little behind, and how far behind depends on the day's wind. The clock at the Tide Office does not strike at all. It keeps its time quietly in a glass case, and it is said to be the most accurate in the city, though nobody can hear it.

The autumn Mira Voss was nine, she spent every evening leaning on the third-floor windowsill, waiting for the two kinds of bell to cross the bay, one after the other. One dropped from Cape Ward like a stone into a well; the other came skimming over the water, always late. How late was different every day.

She wanted to know which of them was wrong.

So one night when both her parents were home late, she carried a stool in from the kitchen, took down the little clock in the sitting room and set it by the kitchen clock on the wall; then she went upstairs to change the mantel clock in the study. Its glass dome was heavy. She held it in both hands and took the stairs holding her breath, afraid it would knock against the banister. By midnight every hand in the three-storey house agreed at last, and all of them were seven minutes slower than before.

In her notebook she wrote: *From today, every clock in this house keeps Pilot Station time.*

The next morning her father missed a conference boat to Ailan.

At breakfast Elias Voss put down his newspaper, looked at his daughter properly for the first time, and asked her why.

Mira talked for twenty minutes. She explained that the clocks on shore and the clocks on ships had never kept the same kind of time; that the tide followed the moon, not the church; that if every observation began from a wrong moment, the numbers after it meant nothing, however handsome they looked. She drew on her napkin as she talked. The butter dish was pushed to the edge of the table. The milk went slowly cold.

When she had finished, her father said, "That's a very interesting question."

He took a card from his wallet and told the driver to take her that afternoon to the best instrument shop in the Inner Harbor, to choose a watch that was truly accurate.

"Leave the ones in the house alone from now on," he said, and got up to catch the next boat.

That night a silver pocket watch was laid beside her pillow. Her name was engraved on the back, the curves of the letters as fine as the lines an ebb tide leaves on sand. It was more accurate than the kitchen clock, more accurate than the church clock, more accurate than the clock at the Pilot Station. She held it to her ear and listened for a long time.

She would keep that watch for many years.

But about those twenty minutes, about which of the two clocks had been wrong, her father never asked again.

That winter she found the answer on her own: neither clock was wrong. The church clock measured the shore; the Pilot Station clock measured the ships, and ships have to wait for the water. She wrote the sentence on the last page of her notebook, and when she had written it she sat for a while, not knowing whom to tell.

In the Voss house, every reasonable request was answered.

If she said she needed a book, it was lying on the hall tray the next morning. If she wanted to see the observatory, the driver was already starting the car at the door. When she ran a high fever in the night, the doctor was climbing the stairs with his bag just as the clock below finished striking two. The house ran like a calibrated tide table: as long as the question was written clearly, the answer arrived on time.

She learned early to write her sentences clearly.

*I'm a little sad today* was not a question that could be handled.

*I would like the third volume of the tidal almanac* was.

Later she seldom said even sentences like that. Whom to invite on her birthday, how many coats to take, how long she would be away: she arranged it all herself. Nobody ever had to remind her about schoolwork; she finished one thing and went on to the next. Like a small boat that needs no tug, she cast off her own lines and left the quay by herself, and was a long way out before anyone on shore turned to look.

Guests at the house often said the Vosses must have put a great deal into this daughter.

That was not wrong.

Nor was it entirely right.
