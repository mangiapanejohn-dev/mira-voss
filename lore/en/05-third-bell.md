# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter V — The Third Bell

Rovelle's harbor has three warning bells.

The first, when it rings, is a reminder: mind the tide. The second restricts the channels: small boats home, large ships under the Pilot Station's orders. The third, which most people in Rovelle hear only a few times in their lives, means the channels are closed, and the low ground goes to higher streets.

The cold current arrived on the night of the fifth day.

The first to notice were the boat repairers at the Old Salt Docks. A few empty buoys that should have drifted out toward the Outer Harbor on the ebb stayed where they were, turning in circles, round and round, as if an invisible hand held them down. The thermometer at the North Reef recorded a sudden drop again, while the tide pole at the South Flats still read: safe.

Rowan sent out the repair boat at low water and it brought back a set of makeshift deep measurements. The instruments were old, the data patchy, and the crew complained on the quay that the compass would not stop shaking. Mira spent a morning piecing the fragments back into the shape of the layers of water, and arrived at a result she did not want to believe easily:

If the northwest wind strengthened before dark, the deep counter-current would pile a wall of water at the harbor mouth. And the tide poles would read it later than the danger itself arrived.

She checked her working through once, then made an estimate by each of two unrelated methods. The two results were of the same size, but almost an hour apart in time.

She could not give an exact hour.

This time she did not hide the uncertain part inside a handsome number.

She took the pages to the Tide Office and asked them to consider ringing the second bell early.

Alden read it through from beginning to end, then called in two senior researchers to check it. One questioned whether the survey boat had been heeling in the swell, so that the temperature readings might be off. Another dug out old records of the White Tide Winter thirty-seven years before, and said a similar change had appeared that year too, and in the end nothing had happened. The argument went on into the afternoon. The man the Harbor Authority had sent kept looking at his watch, its chain swinging against his waistcoat.

"If we close the channel today," he said, "sixty-seven ships will wait in the Outer Harbor until tomorrow night. Who pays for that?"

"I don't know," Mira said.

The room was silent for a moment.

He seemed not to have expected that answer. "Then why are you proposing it?"

"Because I can show you what the risk is if we don't close it," she said. "Who bears the loss is not something I should decide alone."

Alden tapped twice on the table with a knuckle. He knew what came next: the material had to go to the Harbor Authority, the Pilots' Guild, the insurers and the South Flats residents' committee, so that together they could carry a decision that had no perfect answer.

Just then the signal flag at the North Reef was torn in two by the wind, and a telegraph wire came down. The signal from the forward observation post began to come and go.

Mira needed more data.

She thought of the spare thermometer that had just been repaired. She thought of Nell's survey boat. She thought of Nell's exam, which should just have finished.

She could borrow the boat on her own, bring back the data, and tell people the result afterwards. The thought came so naturally that her hand was already reaching for the coat on the stand.

She stopped.

She went into the Pilot Station by the quay. Nell had just come off a boat, her face white from the sea wind, salt in her hair.

"I need you to help me take a measurement," Mira said.

Nell raised her eyebrows. "Now?"

"But first I have to tell you plainly what the risk is. There's already a sideways push in the Outer Harbor, and it may be worse tonight. There has to be a pilot on board who can make the final call. You can say no."

Nell looked at her for a long time.

"Do you know my exam was today?"

"I know," Mira said. "Did you pass?"

"I passed."

At last Mira smiled. "That's good."

"Only good?"

"Very good," she said. "You've waited three years, haven't you?"

Nell couldn't help laughing too. "You really can't talk like a person."

But she did not say yes at once. First she found the duty officer and asked about the state of the boat, the wind, the crew and the conditions for turning back, and she asked a more experienced old pilot to come aboard with them. Mira stood to one side and watched her check each thing in turn, and did not hurry her once.

It was nearly dark when they left harbor. Mira stayed on shore to calculate and record; Nell and the crew took the measurements at sea. Across the darkening water they exchanged numbers by lamp signal. On, off, on.

At seven that evening the last record came back.

The deep counter-current was stronger than she had predicted.

She wrote the revised range of error at the top of the page and signed it. Rowan signed it too. Another researcher added a dissent at the end: the wind could still change direction suddenly before midnight.

The Harbor Authority argued briefly, and decided to pass over the second bell and ring the third. Channel Six was closed, and the residents of the low ground at the South Flats were told to be ready to move.

When the bell dropped from Cape Ward, people on the quay swore. Some swore at the Tide Office, some at the weather. The Merchants' Association demanded a public list of the losses. Someone said this was just one more forecast blown up out of all proportion.

Mira did not argue. She could not promise, herself, that anything would happen that night.

Three hours later, the waves at the North Reef began to change direction.
