# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter III — Four Degrees Below

By the next morning eight years of deep-water temperature records from the North Reef covered Rowan's long table.

Roll lay over roll, held down with teacups, a plumb weight and a broken compass. Most of the lines were orderly, rising and falling with the seasons like a row of people breathing slowly. Only in a very few winters did a curve drop suddenly in a single night, exactly like the roll from the day before.

Mira marked those dates, then went through the port's old records of closures.

She laid the two sets of records side by side and looked at them for a long time.

None of those anomalies had led to an accident, and not because the water was safe. It was because on those days the port happened to be closed for other reasons: once for thick fog, once for a broken crane, and once because the dockers were on strike.

"We have never actually tested the longer opening hours in extreme cold," she said.

Rowan did not look surprised. "You've got this far at last?"

"You knew already?"

"I suspected. There wasn't enough data."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Telling you a suspicion," Rowan said, "and giving you a conclusion you can publish are two different things."

Mira hated it most when he talked in riddles like that. She spread out the last six weeks of records and circled the gaps in red pencil: missing sample points, one after another, like a row of lamps no one had lit.

"The deep layer at the North Reef has to be measured again."

"The instruments are still being repaired."

"We can borrow the set from the school in the Inner Harbor."

"They have their own plans."

"I'll apply."

"Eat first."

"I'm not hungry."

Rowan pointed with his chin at the corner of the table. Lying there was the bread she had brought the day before, gone as dry as a ship's plank.

She stared at it for a while and finally broke it open. She had just taken the first bite when the door opened and Nell came in carrying two cans of hot soup, and a gust of cold air with them.

"Eating stones again?"

"There's salt in it."

"There's salt in stones too."

Nell put one can of soup in front of her and took the other to the window seat. There was a crack in the windowpane, taped over, and it always let in water when it rained.

Nell had been training as a pilot for three years, waiting all that time for a licence to work the open sea. Next Friday she had a practical examination on a working ship; if she passed, she would move from her temporary post at the Old Salt Docks to the big fleet at Ailan.

Mira knew this. It was only that every time, she couldn't remember the date.

"I'd like to borrow your survey boat," Mira said.

"Ask me first which day my exam is."

Mira thought for two seconds. "Next Wednesday?"

"Next Friday."

"Near enough."

"Not near at all."

Nell's voice was still light. Her eyes were not.

Mira put down her spoon. She could hear that something was wrong, but she didn't know which sentence to start mending it from. Nell looked away first and began to talk about her night shift the day before: as the small boat came near the North Reef the water was flat as a mirror, yet something under the hull had pushed it sideways several metres, and her instructor had held every boat back from coming in.

"That could be a deep counter-current." Mira was already drawing on a sheet of paper. "If we could measure twice outside the reef ridge—"

"I understand," Nell said. "Let me ask you one thing first. Could you not treat me like a pair of hands that can row, just for today?"

Mira looked at her.

The sentence was harder to solve than any equation.

"I didn't mean it like that."

"I know you may not have," Nell said. "But whenever you need something, I understand it straight away. When it's my turn, you always think near enough will do."

She didn't wait for an answer. She picked up her soup and left. The door closed behind her, and the wind lifted a corner of the records on the table and let it fall.

Mira sat without moving. At the other end of the long table Rowan kept his head down and polished a brass-cased instrument, very slowly, and said not a word on her behalf.

After a long time she folded the half-finished drawing in two, and in two again, and put it in her pocket.

The soup had gone cold. She drank it to the end, a spoonful at a time.

It was not only a date she had got wrong.
