## Chapter VI — No Perfect Number

Early the next morning there was a new waterline on the outer wall of the lowest warehouse at the South Flats.

It was very clear, as if someone had drawn it along a ruler, and it stood far higher than the mark of any winter tide in past years. If several of the flood gates in the Inner Harbor had not been closed early in the night, the water would have followed the drains into the cellars of the houses.

Some people were relieved, some were angry, and many more cared only when the cargo ships stuck in the Outer Harbor could come in.

The worst outcome in the forecast had not happened. Nor was there any victory worth beating drums for.

An old gate jammed as it was closing and three workmen were crushed and hurt. A supply ship turned aside at short notice and lost half a hold of cargo. Several hundred dockers waited half a night shift in the cold wind for nothing. The warning reached the South Flats too late, and an old man in a wheelchair was carried up to high ground by his neighbours, taking turns.

That morning Mira and Rowan went back to the Tide Office and began comparing what had actually happened with the forecast, item by item.

She had overestimated the water level south of the North Reef and underestimated the local current near the third gate. She wrote both errors into the body of the report, and did not bury them beneath the one line of statistics that said *overall, validated*.

In the afternoon a newspaper reporter found her.

"Did you save the city?"

For a moment she thought he was joking.

"I didn't close the gates," she said. "I didn't take a boat out to sample either. Some people measured, some checked, some decided, some carried it out. Don't write it up as one person's doing."

The reporter asked again, "Do you think you're a genius?"

Mira looked at him for two seconds.

"What does that have to do with last night?"

The next day's paper still printed her name in the most prominent place. But this time the article named many other people, and the Pilots' Guild was mentioned too. Nell read it, snorted, and said the reporters had finally learned to count.

Then the Harbor Authority announced interim measures: the proposal to keep the channel open longer in winter was suspended; Channel Six would get its missing deep observations; the Tide Office had three months to put forward a more reliable warning scheme; and the Harbor Authority itself would publish the economic cost of closing the channel and how it would be compensated.

Alden came to see her and said the Academy hoped she would lead the research that followed.

"I'm about to leave Rovelle," she said.

"I heard."

"Then why have you come to me?"

"Because part of this method is yours," Alden said. "You don't have to stay here to help us check it."

She thought for a while, and agreed to hand over all her records, on one condition: the new research meetings would include pilots, and representatives of the low ground at the South Flats.

Alden did not agree on the spot. He said some procedures would need arranging, and some people might refuse. Mira said fine; she could wait for a formal answer.

The conversation ended quite ordinarily. Nobody finally understood anyone completely, and no official had his view of life changed by a girl of seventeen.

That evening her father came home early. Her mother, for once, had not gone out of town either.

On the table was the baked fish she had loved as a child, and bread cut very thin. Mira stood beside the table for a moment and asked, "Is something special happening today?"

"No," Lena said. "We just wanted to have a meal together."

Halfway through, her mother brought up the offshore research station and said that, if Mira liked, she could find her lodgings, or even go with her and see everything settled.

"I've arranged all of it," Mira said.

"We know."

"Then there's no need—"

"Mira." Her mother stopped her, very quietly. "Sometimes we just don't know what else we can do for you."

Mira looked down at the fish on her plate.

Outside a ship sounded its horn, very far off, very low.

After a long time she said, "There used to be a lot you could have done."

Nobody moved a knife or fork.

She did not say it to hurt anyone. She simply felt, suddenly, that if today she again put *nothing* or *anything's fine* in place of a real answer, this dinner would be no different from any other in the past seventeen years.

Elias set his glass down gently.

"You're right," he said.

"I don't know how to make up for it either," Lena said.

Mira nodded. "Neither do I."

She did not cry, and she did not hug anyone. The three of them stayed where they were and finished the fish.

Later her father asked whether winters in the north needed a heavier coat. She said she had already bought one. Her father was about to let the subject drop when she added:

"There's a big lighthouse next to the station. When I've settled in, I'll send photographs."

Her mother said, "Please."

The moment was small, too small to fill any of the gaps in the past seventeen years.

But nobody was in a hurry to call it a reconciliation, either.
