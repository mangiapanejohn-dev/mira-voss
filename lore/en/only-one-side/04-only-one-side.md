# MØBIUS: Only One Side

> Short story · The first winter · English translation of the Chinese original · v1.0 · 2026-10-21

## IV — Only One Side

On the day they finished reading nineteen winters of logs, the bay began to freeze.

First the shallow water along the shore grew a thin, greyish-white crust that broke whenever a wave came in, and broke, and formed again. By evening the crust reached the third pile of the jetty.

Mira wrote the result on a single sheet of paper.

The cold spikes marked *bleed-through, not counted* numbered forty-six in all. For twenty-nine of them, counting back one whole time round, there was no matching warm trace on the back of the paper. They had nothing to cast them, so they were not shadows. Fourteen really were bleed-through; Kohl had been right about those. The remaining three fell on days when the tape had been spliced, and even Søren could not say how long that time round had been, so all she could write was: *cannot be determined.*

The twenty-nine real cold spikes did not come at random. Most of them crowded around the spring tides, and each year they came a little earlier.

"The cold tongue," Ada said when she had read it. That was all she said.

It was the cold water the captain of the supply ship had spoken of: reaching out from Ice Bay, creeping south along the seabed, and going a little further at every spring tide. Everyone at the station knew about it, yet nobody had ever looked for it in the deep-water record, because every place in the record where it might have been had been marked *not counted*.

Ada did not believe it straight away. She gave the positions of the forty-six spikes to Jens, another researcher at the station, without telling him Mira's conclusion, and had him count them again from the beginning on his own.

Three days later Jens's result came back: twenty-eight real, fifteen bleed-through, three that could not be determined.

On one of them, the two of them disagreed.

Mira and Jens spent a whole afternoon holding that length of tape up to the lamp, front and back, under a magnifying glass, and in the end neither persuaded the other. In the report she wrote: *twenty-eight to twenty-nine real signals, one of them disputed*, and gave each judgement's reasons.

"You don't want to settle it?" Ada asked.

"It can't be settled," Mira said. "I could pick one. But then the report would be more certain than the tape."

Ada looked at her for a while. Then she picked up her pencil and moved the deep-water probe that was to have gone to the fourth question mark to the edge of the trench the cold tongue ran along.

The station had only one spare probe left. Mira thought of the set of surveying instruments imported from the south, at the very bottom of her case: her father's present when she was thirteen, which she had never been able to bring herself to take to sea. That night she took it out and gave it to Søren.

Over the names on the report, she and Søren argued for an entire evening.

She wanted Søren's name first. Søren said he had done no analysis; he had only read the logs aloud. Mira said that without those logs her analysis was only a handsome guess. Ilse listened for a while and said the two of them argued in exactly the same way: each would only talk about what the other had done.

In the end, the first page of the report said:

*Records: Søren Aske (winters thirteen to thirty-one)*
*Analysis: Mira Voss*
*Check: Jens Ottesen*

She did not cross out the three words. Under Kohl's note she began a new line: *Of these, fourteen to fifteen judged correctly. For the rest, see this report.*

The same week a form came from the planning office in Ailan. The winter deep-water survey had to be registered formally, and the station needed a name. The lighthouse had a name, Vard Light; but the station had never had a name of its own. People only called it *the station by the lighthouse*.

At dinner everyone joked about it. Ilse said to call it Cat Station, since the cat was its most senior member. The people in the kitchen said Seven Minutes Slow Station. Jens said the longer the name, the harder the winter.

Ada turned and asked Mira what she thought.

Mira thought for a while, and thought of the loop in the instrument hut with its half twist.

"That tape," she said, "looks as if it has two sides, but it only has one. Start from any point and keep going forward, and without ever crossing an edge you come to its back. Keep going, and you're where you started."

"In the north they call a loop like that a *møbius*," Søren said. He made the *ø* very round, as if he had a piece of ice in his mouth. "The old boat repairers all joined their belts that way. Both sides wear together, so it lasts."

Ada took the form and wrote in pencil: MØBIUS.

"That'll do for now," she said. "We'll change it later."

The name was never changed.

That night it was Mira's turn on watch.

The instrument hut was very quiet; there was only the generator humming low in the distance. The tape moved slowly between the two drums, and the recording pen swung lightly against the paper, writing the temperature of the seabed at that moment. The clock on the wall was still seven minutes slow. Beside the line of pencil under it, rubbed shiny by fingers, there was a new line, in her hand:

*Known. Recorded.*

She watched the tape go round the far drum, turn over by half a twist, and the pen come down on what looked like the back and go on writing.

When she left Rovelle, she had thought she was turning over to the other side. Her home, her parents, Nell, the Tide Office and the unfinished chart were all left on the back, and what she would write from now on was a completely new page.

Now she no longer thought so.

There was no other side. Rovelle was still on this tape, in the places she had already passed. The clocks, the dinner table, the jacket that smelled of tung oil were all still there.

She had only gone a long way along the same side.

Outside the window the light of the lighthouse swept across once more, lit the frozen bay, and let it go back into the dark.

**The End**
