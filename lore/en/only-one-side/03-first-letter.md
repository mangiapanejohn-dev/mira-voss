# MØBIUS: Only One Side

> Short story · The first winter · English translation of the Chinese original · v1.0 · 2026-10-21

## III — The First Letter

For the next ten days Mira put the records in order by day and went to the instrument hut at night.

Søren read the logs aloud and she wrote. Which day a new roll went on; which day ice cut the tape and a length was spliced in; which day the generator stopped for four hours and the pen stood still and bled a round blot of ink. The northern shorthand came out of his mouth as clear lines of dates and times, like water under ice when a hole is cut, welling slowly up.

One winter, he had written only one line in his log:

*Cat ill. No sleep. Paper as usual.*

Mira copied that line down too. Søren asked her what she was copying it for.

"The record that day might not be accurate."

"The record that day is very accurate," Søren said. "I wasn't."

She thought about it and added a note beside it: *Recorder did not sleep. Pen trace normal.*

Søren glanced at it and did not object.

On the morning of the eleventh day Ilse stopped her in the corridor and pointed at the duty board on the wall.

It was an old wooden board. Everyone's name was written on a small wooden tag and hung on one hook or another: night weather readings, generator rounds, kitchen, clearing ice from the aerial. Mira's tag had been hung up on her second day at the station, in the square for *night readings*.

She had never once seen it.

"I did two of your night shifts," Ilse said. "I'm not asking you to pay them back. It's just that here, nobody else is going to do them."

Mira stood in front of the board, her face growing hot by degrees.

In Rovelle there were many things she never needed to see. The book would come the next day, the car would wait at the door, dinner would be cooked and on the table. She had always believed she was independent because nobody ever had to remind her about schoolwork. Only today did she understand that part of her *independence* came from someone always finishing the other things, somewhere she couldn't see.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I'll take the next two night shifts."

"One's enough."

"Two."

Ilse looked at her and suddenly laughed. "Do you haggle like this in Rovelle?"

"In Rovelle, other people haggle with me."

"Then you're learning fast."

The supply ship arrived on the thirteenth day. The ice had not yet closed the bay. The captain said this would probably be the last run before the year ended.

There were three letters for her on board.

The first was from her mother. Wrapped in brown paper was a very thick scarf, grey-blue, and a sheet of paper written to the edges: how to keep the damp out of northern houses, what to put on chilblains, which kind of wool doesn't scratch your neck. The last line was in smaller writing than the rest:

*You probably know all this. I just wanted to write it.*

The second was from her father, and very short.

*I have read your technical report. I didn't understand the assumption on page eleven. If you have time, you could explain it to me. If you don't, that's all right.*

Mira looked at these few lines for a long time. Her father's handwriting was very neat, the hand of a man who signs insurance policies, every stroke inside its box. Only *that's all right* was written a little crooked.

The third was from Nell. In the top right-hand corner of the page, in heavy strokes, was the date:

*19 December. This time I wrote it down first.*

The letter itself was only half a page. The captain of the new fleet had a foul temper but never shouted at the wrong person. The third observation station at the North Reef had been measured again once; the data were copied on the back. At the bakery at the Old Salt Docks she had bought Mira a pancake so sweet it hurt, and eaten it herself. *Ate it for you. No need to thank me.*

Mira stacked the three letters together, weighed them down under the desk lamp, and began to write back.

She wrote to her father first. She explained the assumption on page eleven in two and a half pages with three diagrams; when she had finished she read it from the beginning and cut two passages of working he probably would not need. Then, at the end, she wrote one more paragraph, very slowly:

*There are ten people here in winter, and a cat. One of the station's clocks is seven minutes slow, and nobody sets it right, because setting it right would mean changing twenty years of records. I think you'd like the reason. A few days ago I forgot my night shift, and someone else did it for me. I never used to notice things like that. Now I'm noticing.*

She looked at the last sentence and thought it was not well written. But it was true.

The letter to Nell began:

*Why does the captain of the new fleet have a foul temper?*

Only after that line did she write about the data from the North Reef.

The letter to her mother she began three times. The first time she wrote: *Everything's fine.*

She looked at the two words. Many years ago she had said *No* to her father, just as cleanly, just as quickly.

She crossed them out.

In the end she wrote: *The scarf is very warm. The wool doesn't scratch. I don't know yet what winter here will be like. When I do, I'll tell you.*

She borrowed the station's camera from Ilse. The two of them climbed the lighthouse against the wind, the spiral stair going round and round, the iron rail so cold it stuck to their hands. On the top platform the wind was so strong it was hard to stand. Mira held up the camera, her hands shaking all the while, and just as she pressed the shutter the light swept round.

When the photograph was developed it was a blur of white.

She put it in the envelope to her parents anyway, and wrote on the back:

*The lighthouse is inside that white patch on the left.*
