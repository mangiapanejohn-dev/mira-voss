# MØBIUS: Only One Side

> Short story · The first winter · English translation of the Chinese original · v1.0 · 2026-10-21

## I — Arrival

North from Ailan, the supply ship took three days.

On the first day the coast was still in sight, line after line of black reef, as if someone had traced the edge of the sky in charcoal. On the second there was only grey water and a greyer sky, and the crew coiled the ropes on deck, round and round, very carefully, like putting away a winter coat that would not be needed for a while. On the evening of the third day, Mira saw a beam of light outside the porthole.

It swept across, and after a while swept across again. And again, after a while.

"Vard Light," the captain said. "Once you can see it, you're there."

"How much further?"

"Two hours. Or four."

Mira looked at him. The captain did not explain. He only pushed a cup of hot tea across to her, a fine sheen of oil floating on its surface.

Later she learned that a long band of cold water lay along the seabed here, reaching out from Ice Bay and creeping south along the bottom. Ships passing over it went sometimes faster, sometimes slower. No northern captain would ever give the time of landing as a single number.

She liked that answer. At least it didn't pretend to be accurate.

The ship came alongside at nine that night.

The jetty was very short, and its piles were wrapped in white frost. The station was smaller than she had imagined: three stone-walled houses with timber roofs, huddled together out of the wind; a generator shed, humming; an instrument hut that reached out into the water, with windows on all four sides. The lighthouse stood on the highest point of the cape, its white tower worn grey by the wind, and every twelve seconds the light at its top swept over everyone's heads.

The person who met her was a young woman not many years older than she was, wrapped in an oilskin coat far too big for her, with ice in the brim of her hat. She said her name was Ilse, and that she ran the radio and the weather reports.

"Dr Brenner says to tell you seven o'clock tomorrow morning." Ilse took one of her cases and weighed it in her hand. "What have you got in here, stones?"

"Charts."

"Heavier than stones, then."

They climbed the frozen path. The light of the lighthouse passed overhead, stretching their two shadows long and drawing them back again.

"How many people are here in winter?" Mira asked.

"Nine. Ten, with you." Ilse thought about it. "Eleven, with Søren's cat. The cat came before you, so it ranks above you."

Mira didn't laugh, and she didn't feel slighted either. In Rovelle people always knew her name before they saw her. Here nobody mentioned the tide chart, and nobody asked what it was like to do research at seventeen. She was simply the tenth person, ranked below a cat.

It made her feel very light. As if she had put down a case she had carried for a long time without ever weighing it.

Her room was on the first floor of the second house, with a window facing the sea. She set down her cases and, without turning on the lamp, stood at the window for a while.

The light of the lighthouse swept the sea in long strokes, lighting a stretch of waves and letting it go back into the dark. Further off was a low lamp that did not turn with the lighthouse. It stayed lit, quietly, close to the water.

That was the instrument hut.

Toward midnight the lamp was still on.

Mira put on her coat, went downstairs and walked out along the icy boardwalk. The door of the instrument hut stood ajar, and inside it smelled of machine oil, old paper and damp wool. A man with completely white hair had his back to the door and was taking a length of paper tape off a drum. He moved very slowly, like someone opening a letter he did not want to tear.

The tape was very wide. It went round two drums at either end of the hut and was drawn out under the lamp into a long loop. A recording pen rested on the paper, swinging gently from side to side with the signals that came up from the probe on the seabed, like someone writing in their sleep.

Mira watched for a while and felt that something was not right.

As the tape went round the far drum, it turned over by half a twist.

"That joins the front to the back," she said.

The man did not turn round. "It does."

"The pen will write on the back."

"One time round, it writes on the front. The next time round, on the back." He rolled up the tape he had taken off and tied it with a thin cord. "One roll of paper lasts twice as many days. In winter the supply ship may not get through. The paper mustn't run out first."

Only then did he turn and look at her. The lamp glinted in his spectacles.

"You're the one from the Tide Office."

"I'm not from the Tide Office."

"They said you were."

"They were wrong."

The man seemed to find that answer acceptable, and gave one nod. "Søren," he said, and lifted his chin toward the wall. "That clock is seven minutes slow. Leave it alone."

Mira looked up. On the wall hung a round clock in a brass frame, and its hands were indeed seven minutes behind her pocket watch.

She put her hand in her pocket and felt the silver watch that kept far better time than the kitchen clock ever had, and suddenly she very much wanted to laugh.

"Why don't you set it right?"

"If I did, every page of the last twenty years of records would need a note added," Søren said. "If I don't, it needs one note: *seven minutes slow*."

He showed her. The note was written in pencil on the board under the clock, the letters rubbed shiny by fingers, one generation after another.

Outside the window the light of the lighthouse swept across again. The tape moved slowly between the two drums, went round the half twist, and the pen came down on the other side and went on writing.
