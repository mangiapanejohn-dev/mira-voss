# MØBIUS: Only One Side

> Short story · The first winter · English translation of the Chinese original · v1.0 · 2026-10-21

## II — Bleed-Through

Ada Brenner was a person who talked fast and wrote slowly.

By seven in the morning she had spread a plan of the winter survey over one end of the long table in the mess and weighed it down with four enamel mugs. Six deep-water stations were marked on it. Three had ticks; beside the other three were question marks she had pencilled in, very, very small.

"Once Ice Bay freezes, no boat gets out," she said. "We have about three weeks to get the probes down. Once they're down, they stay where they are all winter."

"What are the three question marks?" Mira asked.

"The places I'm not sure of yet." Ada pushed a cup of coffee across to her. "Your job isn't to find anything new. First put the deep-water records of the last twenty winters in order and tell me what normal is."

Mira had come prepared with many questions. When she heard that, she didn't ask a single one.

In Rovelle, Rowan had said much the same. At the time she had thought it was stalling. Now she knew that some people say *first find out what's normal* because they have seen too many abnormal things taken for normal.

The records room was above the instrument hut, narrow and cold. One wooden crate for each winter, and in each crate the tapes rolled up that year and a thin annual report. The tapes were heavier than the reports; the reports were easier to read.

She read the reports first. By the fourth winter she had found a note that kept coming back.

Every few days a downward spike appeared in the deep-water temperature curve. Very short, very cold, as if someone had jabbed the paper hard with the tip of a pen. And beside every spike were the same three words:

*Bleed-through, not counted.*

The words first appeared nineteen years earlier. That year a visiting researcher named Kohl had come to the station. His report put it plainly: the tape was joined in a loop with a half twist, and the pen's marks soaked through the thin paper, so that on the next time round they left a shadow from the back. Those regular cold spikes were shadows, not sea.

For the nineteen years after that, the three words were copied down, year after year. In the end even the reason was left out.

Mira carried the tape of the eleventh winter downstairs and unrolled a length of it on the long table.

The paper was very thin. Held up to the window, the writing on the back really could be seen, faint, as if through a layer of fog. Kohl had not been wrong. The bleed-through was real.

But she looked at that length of paper for a long time and remembered the half twist on the far drum the night before.

When the tape turned over by half a twist, it was not only front and back that changed places.

Left and right changed too.

The edge that had been on the left came round again on the right. Where the pen fell on the paper stood for temperature: the further left, the colder; the further right, the warmer. So a cold mark that soaked through to the back should not still be on the cold side when the pen came over it again on the next time round.

It should appear on the warm side.

Bleed-through would turn cold into warm and warm into cold.

If these cold spikes were all shadows, then one whole time round before each of them, on the back of the paper, there should be a warm spike: the thing that cast it.

She found the first cold spike, counted back one time round, and turned the paper over.

There.

The second. Nothing.

The third, nothing. The fourth, there.

By noon she had checked eleven. For seven of them, the back of the paper one time round earlier was clean. There was nothing there at all.

Mira put down her pencil and found that her fingertips were a little cold. She knew the feeling. A few months before, at Rowan's long table, looking at three numbers that contradicted one another, it had been like this.

She had already begun writing a note to Ada on a sheet of paper: *Of the cold spikes struck out as bleed-through over the past nineteen years, at least some are a real ocean signal, possibly related to—*

Her pen stopped there.

How long one time round was depended on how long the tape was. The tape had broken, been spliced, been changed for a new roll, and every time the place of *one whole time round* moved a little forward or back. For the eleven she had just checked, she had used a length she had assumed herself. If the tape had been spliced even once partway through that year, some of her seven might be her own miscounts.

The reports did not say on which day the tape had been spliced.

But there was one person who would certainly know.

When she found Søren in the instrument hut he was changing the ink in the recording pen. The cat sat on a crate beside the drum, tail wrapped round its feet, watching the tape move forward an inch at a time, like a fish that would never swim away.

"I'd like to see your logs," Mira said.

"Why?"

She laid the length of tape and her own sketch in front of him and explained, from the half twist to left and right changing places, from eleven spikes to seven shadows that had nothing to cast them. She talked very fast, and only when she had finished did she notice that Søren had not interrupted her once.

He picked up the sketch and looked at it for a long time.

"Nineteen years ago," he said, "I told Kohl some of them weren't bleed-through."

Mira looked up.

"What did he say?"

"He said paper lies." Søren put the sketch back on the table. "So do people."

"You didn't raise it again?"

"I repair the instruments, change the paper, and write down when I changed it," he said. "The conclusions are what you people write."

His voice was level; there was no complaint to be heard in it. But Mira could hear that he had kept that sentence in him for nineteen years.

She thought of what Rowan had said: telling you a suspicion and giving you a conclusion you can publish are two different things. She had hated that sentence at the time. Only now did she see its other side: some suspicions never get the chance to grow into conclusions, only because the person who speaks them is not the one who writes the reports.

"I could read the logs slowly on my own," she said. "But I'd much rather ask you to read them with me. Which year the paper was spliced, which times even you can't be sure of: you know that better than I do. Whatever we write in the end should carry your name."

Søren looked at her as if estimating the error of a new instrument.

"Names later," he said. "First see whether it's true."

That night he dragged a tin trunk out from under his bed. Thirty-one hardbound logs, one for each winter, in northern shorthand, the letters as small as ants. Their edges had been soaked in seawater and dried again by the stove, and they felt like bark.

Mira could not read a single page.

She found she didn't mind at all.
