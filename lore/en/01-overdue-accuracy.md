# Rovelle: The Unfinished Chart

> Book One · English translation of the Chinese original · v3.0 · 2026-10-21

## Chapter I — Overdue Accuracy

The invitation came six weeks after her seventeenth birthday.

Two sheets of thin paper in an envelope gone soft with rain, the gum along the flap puckered, as if it had had a hard journey down from the north. The first sheet said she had been accepted for the winter deep-water survey at an offshore research station. The second listed the date of arrival and the papers to bring, and at the very bottom, in small print: *Winter supplies are irregular. Bring plenty of paper.*

Mira read it on the tram to the Old Salt Docks, folded it in four and put it in the inside pocket of her coat.

The tram swayed across the bridge over the Inner Harbor. The water outside was grey-green, like a pane of glass nobody had wiped properly. A month ago she had made plans for the case where the letter said no: another season of measurements at the Tide Office, another station next year. Now the letter had come, and the first thought that rose in her made no sense at all.

If she left in December, she would miss the Buoy Festival at the Old Salt Docks.

There was nothing at the Buoy Festival she especially liked. The best stall sold pancakes so sweet they hurt, and the wind on the river wall went straight through your ears. She had been many times and had been cold nearly every time.

"What are you looking at?" Nell leaned over from the seat behind, chin propped on the back of Mira's seat.

Mira handed her the letter.

Halfway through, Nell whistled. "That's not an easy letter to get."

"I know."

"And that's it?"

"What should it be?"

"You could be happy. For instance."

Mira thought about it and lifted both corners of her mouth.

Nell slapped the letter back into her hand. "Forget it. That's horrible."

The air at the Old Salt Docks always held three smells: swollen timber, bread just out of the oven, and, underneath them both and never quite gone, fish. They walked along the stone wall outside the boat sheds toward the observatory. Halfway there Nell was caught by an old man selling used screws; she crouched down and picked through them one at a time, haggling over each one. Mira didn't hurry her. She leaned on the iron rail of the quay and watched a cargo ship back out of its berth.

The stern swung half a metre off. The helmsman corrected it, and it swung half a metre off again. The hawser pulled straight and gave a very low note, as if something under the water had hold of it.

"Why is it so slow today?" she asked.

"Odd currents out by the North Reef." Nell didn't look up. "Two ships were an hour and a half late yesterday. The Pilot Station says it's only the wind."

Only the wind.

In Rovelle that is a dangerous comfort. It may be true. It may also be just a word everyone agrees to use for a while, until somebody finds a better explanation.

The observatory stood at the end of the stone wall, a small draughty building whose window frames the salt had eaten into a white rim. When they pushed the door open, Rowan Venn was bent over the long table repairing a jammed water-temperature recorder, scraping the salt crust off its casing a little at a time with a pocket knife. The white scrapings lay heaped on a sheet of paper like a small drift of snow.

"Don't touch anything yet." He didn't look up. "The Tide Office is going to keep Channel Six open two hours longer in winter."

Mira stopped.

"Who approved that?"

"They're still meeting. The Merchants' Association says your new folding tide chart has made the ordinary return currents predictable enough."

"That chart doesn't cover extreme winter cold."

"I know."

"The report says so plainly."

Only then did Rowan raise his eyes and look at her over his spectacles. "Not everyone reads the report."

There was nothing in the room but the sound of the knife.

The year before, as its lead author, she had reworked that chart. The old one folded the speeds of the water at different depths into a single average, and once a channel narrowed it was easy to misread. The new one kept the deep observations separate, and it had been used on six trial routes for a whole year. In ordinary seas the warnings came earlier and more accurately than before, and there were fewer needless closures. It was the first time she had a name in this work that would bear weight.

The last page of the report was just as plain: *Deep sampling is limited. Once the temperature structure falls outside the trial range, the error cannot be estimated in the usual way.*

Now someone wanted to use that chart to prove the winter sea could stay open two hours longer.

"They can't use it like that," she said.

"You can lodge an objection first."

"I'll go straight to the Harbor Authority."

"First finish reading yesterday's raw records."

Rowan pushed a roll of chart paper toward her. Its edges had been soaked in seawater and dried stiff, and rolled up it looked like a length of bark.

She unrolled it and weighed down the four corners with the ink bottle and a paperweight.

Three rows of figures. The surface current very slow. The water level rising and falling normally. The deep water temperature dropping four degrees in less than half a day.

She put her finger on the four.

"This isn't right."

"Which part?"

"All of it," she said. "They shouldn't happen at the same time."

"The thermometer could be broken."

"Then prove it's broken first."

Outside the window, the signal flag over Channel Six turned slowly half a circle in the wind, and changed direction.
